# Scouting: the rest of ASEAN

Singapore, Cambodia, Lao PDR, Myanmar, Brunei Darussalam and Timor-Leste. Scouted on 2026-11-04 for a later expansion phase ([ROADMAP.md](../ROADMAP.md)). The Philippines, Thailand and Malaysia are being scouted separately.

**How to read this.** This is desk research from web searches and official pages fetched where the sites allowed it. Nothing here has been checked against the binding text the way phase 2 requires. Each rule has a confidence level:

- **high**: the official page or document was read, or several credible sources agree on the date and effect.
- **med**: secondary sources agree, but the official text was not read.
- **low**: one source only, sources conflict, or the instrument (its number, or whether it exists) is not confirmed.

Rule counts are rough estimates of rows worth adding under the current scope and topic list. They are not counts of instruments.

**Timor-Leste is now a full member.** It was admitted as ASEAN's 11th member on **26 October 2025**, at the 47th ASEAN Summit in Kuala Lumpur, under the Declaration on the Admission of Timor-Leste into ASEAN. It had been an observer since 2022. At the 43rd ASEAN Ministers on Energy Meeting (16 October 2025, ten days before admission) it still took part as an observer. Ministers welcomed progress on its Instrument of Accession to ASEAN's energy-related legal instruments, which has not been completed (sources: Vietnam News, AMEM joint statement; confidence high).

## Singapore

**Structure.** Generation is fully private and about 95% of electricity comes from gas. SP Group owns the transmission and distribution grid and runs it. EMA is the regulator, the power system operator and the planner. Energy Market Company (EMC) runs the wholesale market, and the retail Open Electricity Market has been open to households since 2018–19. On 1 October 2026 MTI was renamed the **Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry (METI)**, with a minister for energy and industry. EMA now sits under METI. Carbon pricing sits with NCCS (in the Prime Minister's Office) and NEA.

**Legal hierarchy.** Acts of Parliament (Electricity Act 2001, EMA Act 2001, Gas Act 2001, Carbon Pricing Act 2018) → subsidiary legislation (regulations, orders, commencement notifications; numbered "S xx/YYYY") → EMA codes of practice and market rules (Transmission Code, Metering Code, Market Support Services Code, Singapore Electricity Market Rules) → EMA determinations and policy papers, RFPs and licence conditions.

**Sources.** Singapore Statutes Online (sso.agc.gov.sg) holds the consolidated Acts and subsidiary legislation with commencement history. It is authoritative but returns 403 to scripts, so any portal scan needs a browser or a manual step. EMA publishes media releases, consultations and final determinations as PDFs on ema.gov.sg. The site is JavaScript-rendered, but direct PDF links download. NCCS publishes the carbon tax rate schedule.

## Cambodia

**Structure.** The state utility Electricité du Cambodge (EDC) is the single buyer and main distributor, alongside hundreds of licensed rural distributors. The Electricity Authority of Cambodia (EAC), created by the 2001 Electricity Law, issues licences, sets tariffs and writes regulations. The Ministry of Mines and Energy (MME) sets policy and plans, and issues prakas. Supply comes from hydro, coal, fast-growing utility-scale solar, and imports from Lao PDR, Thailand and Vietnam. More than 60% of generation is renewable (reported in 2026).

**Legal hierarchy.** Law (Chbab), promulgated by Royal Kram → Royal Decree (Preah Reach Kret) → Sub-decree (Anukret) → Ministerial Prakas (proclamation) and Decision (Sechkdei Samrech) → EAC regulations, procedures and decisions (for example tariff decisions numbered by EAC session).

**Sources.** The EAC regulations page (https://eac.gov.kh/site/regulation?lang=en) is the best official portal found in this scouting. It lists the Electricity Law (as amended in 2007 and 2015), sub-decrees, MME prakas (including the rooftop solar quotas for 2024, 2025 and 2026) and EAC regulations, many with official English PDFs. It also has tariff decision lists and annual "Salient Features" reports. MME (https://mme.gov.kh/) is mostly in Khmer. Open Development Cambodia indexes prakas with dates and summaries in English.

## Lao PDR

**Structure.** Électricité du Laos (EDL) is the state single buyer and distributor. Its finances are weak: it carries an estimated US$8bn of debt on top of sovereign debt. Since 2021 EDL-Transmission (EDL-T), about 90% owned by China Southern Power Grid, has run the national grid under a 25-year concession. Most capacity is hydro built by IPPs for export to Thailand and Vietnam. There is no independent regulator. In 2025 the Ministry of Energy and Mines was merged into the **Ministry of Industry and Commerce (MOIC)**: the National Assembly approved it on 20 March 2025, and the merger was completed in June 2025.

**Legal hierarchy.** Law (National Assembly; promulgated by Presidential Decree) → Decree (Prime Minister or Government) → Decision, Agreement and Instruction (Prime Minister or ministry) → notices. Party and National Assembly resolutions set direction: see the PLAN.md note on party resolutions.

**Sources.** The Lao Official Gazette, run by the Ministry of Justice, did not respond to requests from this environment. Open Development Laos and UNEP LEAP carry unofficial English versions of the Electricity Law. The state news agency KPL (kpl.gov.la, which returned 503 at times) and Vientiane Times report decisions.

## Myanmar

**Structure.** The Ministry of Electric Power (MOEP) owns generation (EPGE) and distribution enterprises (YESC, MESC and others), with hydro (about 51%), gas, LNG and a few IPPs. The State Administration Council split the Ministry of Electricity and Energy into MOEP and a Ministry of Energy on 2 May 2022 (SAC Order 33/2022). After the 2021 coup the sector faces fuel shortages and rolling blackouts: rotating outages were reintroduced from 18 March 2026. Elections ran from December 2025 to January 2026, a USDP-led parliament elected Min Aung Hlaing president on 3 April 2026, and a 31-ministry government was announced on 10 April 2026. Most Western governments treat these institutions as illegitimate.

**Legal hierarchy.** Pyidaungsu Hluttaw laws (in 2021–26 replaced by SAC laws) → rules and notifications by the Union Government or a ministry → ministry orders, directives and announcements (tariffs).

**Sources.** moep.gov.mm is up, but older content links return 404 (moee.gov.mm now redirects there). Myanmar Law Information System did not respond. Law-firm alerts (DFDL), Eleven Myanmar and Open Development Myanmar are the practical sources.

## Brunei Darussalam

**Structure.** Two vertically integrated utilities: the Department of Electrical Services (DES), which is state-owned and supplies about 60% of demand, and Berakas Power Company (BPC), a separate network in Brunei-Muara. Generation is almost entirely gas. AENBD (Autoriti Elektrik Negara Brunei Darussalam) enforces the Electricity Order 2017. The Ministry of Energy was abolished in May 2022, and policy returned to a **Department of Energy in the Prime Minister's Office**. Climate policy sits with the Brunei Climate Change Secretariat.

**Legal hierarchy.** Orders made under the Constitution (the main legislation, since there is no elected legislature) → revised as Acts (Electricity Act, Chapter 223) → regulations and notifications → directives, guidelines and white papers.

**Sources.** AGC's Laws of Brunei (agc.gov.bn) for Orders and revised Acts; Department of Energy (energy.gov.bn) for guidebooks; climatechange.gov.bn (did not respond from here) for BNCCP and the GHG directive.

## Timor-Leste

**Structure.** **Full ASEAN member since 26 Oct 2025.** EDTL, E.P. (a public company created in 2020) generates, transmits and distributes power, mainly from the Hera and Betano heavy-fuel and diesel plants. The regulator is the Autoridade Nacional para a Eletricidade (ANE, I.P., created in 2020), which still depends on the state budget. The Ministry of Public Works is responsible for the sector. The first IPP is the Laleia (Manatuto) 72 MW solar plant with battery storage, built by Manatuto Renewable Power (Itochu and EDF). Its PPA was signed in July 2025, ground was broken on 23 July 2026, and building takes 18–24 months.

**Legal hierarchy.** Law (National Parliament) → Decree-Law (Government) → Government Decree and Resolution → ministerial diploma. All are published in the Jornal da República (Série I).

**Sources.** Jornal da República PDF archive (mj.gov.tl/jornal); the Asia-Pacific Energy Portal for key texts; the government portal (timor-leste.gov.tl) for Council of Ministers communiqués; Tatoli (the state news agency).

## Recommended order after the Philippines and Thailand

1. **Singapore.** English binding texts, a stable portal and high regional relevance. Its import licences and carbon credit rules are the other side of Indonesian, Lao and Cambodian rules already or soon to be tracked. The main work is a schema extension for regulator determinations and codes.
2. **Cambodia.** The EAC portal is unusually good and often bilingual. Rooftop solar rules change every year (principles, a regulation, annual quotas), which shows off amendment chains and gives a strong rooftop-solar comparison with Vietnam and Indonesia.
3. **Lao PDR.** It matters for cross-border trade and LTMS-PIP. Wait for the National Assembly to adopt the amended Electricity Law (approved in principle in August 2026) and start with it and its implementing decrees, so most rows are not quickly superseded. It needs a Lao-reading partner.
4. **Brunei + Timor-Leste**, as light profiles of 5–10 rows each. Brunei is English and quick. Timor-Leste is a new member with a regime that is being built now (ANE, the first IPP) and Portuguese texts. Together they finish ASEAN-11 coverage cheaply.
5. **Myanmar**, last and as a watch list: framework law, tariffs and institutional changes only, with a clear note on who issued each rule and on contested legitimacy.

**Hardest:** Myanmar (legitimacy, access, Burmese, old content disappearing) and Lao PDR (Lao-only texts, unreachable gazette, decisions made through contracts and cabinet decisions, and a framework about to change). Easiest: Singapore and Brunei.
